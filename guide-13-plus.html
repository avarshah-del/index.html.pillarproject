Read the metaphor, then decide what you can actually carry

You are old enough to hear the limit of the story. In Case Alzheimer's, pressing the orange button and shouting ZAP does not pull the weeds out. The golden screens play older memories instead. A hug is not a treatment. It is still one of the only gadgets the book and the Training Zone agree on, because feelings remain real when the files do not.

Case Alzheimer's is written for ages 6–12. This page is a companion for teens and youth allies, not a claim that the book was written for them. It translates the book's pictures into general facts families already live with. It does not describe stages, medicines, or what will happen to a specific person. Alzheimer's, caregiving, and the metaphor's edge

The disease, without a lecture

Alzheimer's is a brain disease. Over time it commonly makes new memories harder to keep, word-finding harder (the book's aphasia), mood and fear harder to steady (Emotion-Land, the amygdala), and planning harder (Brain HQ, the prefrontal cortex, where even a cookie recipe can go missing). The glossary's tangles — twisted proteins that block tracks like weeds — are one real change the story draws. Plaques, named on the book page as part of what readers learn, are another: clumps between cells. The plot follows the weeds. It does not show a medical timeline, and neither should you invent one for a younger cousin.

Recent files fail first. That is not the whole disease.

The Training Zone teaches that new memories are usually the ones lost first, while older favorites can still be reached — the Deep Archives, visited with photo albums and songs. Hold that as an early, useful pattern, not a promise. As the disease continues, older memories can become harder to find too. Love is not stored in a cabinet the weeds cannot touch. The book's comfort is narrower and truer: the person is still there, and a familiar song can be a better door than a quiz.

Caregiving is the other plot

Our Story is about Ava's grandfather, Jayant Shah, and her grandmother, Hina, who became his primary caregiver. The page is honest that he became confused and distressed, and eventually much less active, and that the years were harsh on the person doing the care. The line they keep is simple: caregivers cannot pour from an empty cup. The Pillar Project's whole "Divide & Pamper" model exists so a primary caregiver can leave for a while without the person they love being left alone or unoccupied. If your house has a Hina in it — a parent, an aunt, a grandparent doing the daily work — the mission is partly theirs to rest, not only the patient's to be entertained.

Your feelings count, including the ugly ones

Embarrassment when a grandparent repeats a story in front of friends, anger when a parent is too tired to show up for you, guilt for wanting to leave the room: those live in your amygdala too. The Training Zone says feelings stay real. That includes yours. You are not required to be the calm one every hour. You are also not the doctor, the spouse, or the only caregiver. Saying "I need a break" is part of co-regulation, not a betrayal. Co-regulation, on the home page, means gentle sensory engagement and shared calm — a high-school ally helping the room settle. It is not taking control of another person's body or their medical decisions.

What not to promise a younger kid

Do not say the weeds come out if we love hard enough. Do not say "they're already gone" because a new fact did not stick. Do not post someone's confused moment. Do not correct harshly. If a younger sibling is reading with you, use the Early Readers guide or the Under 5 guide and let ZAP mean "the old movies still play," which is what the picture shows.

At home, and out in the community Step 2 · Right-sized help

Step 2 · Right-sized help

At home, and out in the community

Pick one. A single reliable hour beats a speech about changing memory care.

At home

Be the second person in the room

  • Answer a repeated question again, in the same calm voice. The New Data door did not file it. Quizzing will not make it stick.
  • Offer a photo album or a song they already know. That is the Training Zone's advice for the Data Library, and it matches the Deep Archives better than a memory test.
  • For something to do together, open the HQ Rec Room. It is the site's shelf of intergenerational activities. Choose the one that fits the person and the day.
  • Hand the primary caregiver a real window — a walk, a nap, an errand — while you stay. That is Divide & Pamper at kitchen-table size. The site describes respite as about one to two hours, not a personality transplant.
In the community

Volunteer only if the age and the waiver fit

  • The volunteer page accepts high school and college students, ages 14–22. Under 18, a parent or guardian signs a liability waiver before the first visit.
  • The path is apply, shadow a session, then lead. Allies run art, music, and tactile sensory sessions at partner communities and give family caregivers a one-to-two-hour break. Service hours can be signed off for school.
  • A certificate is optional for showing up at home. The Get certified list is what we checked. The volunteer page still decides what training is required before you lead events at another facility.
  • If you are 13, or 14 and not ready, you are not late. Home help still counts. Do not borrow a birthday to get into a memory-care unit.
If someone needs support tonight The chat on this site already points families to the Alzheimer's NJ Helpline at 888-280-6055. The Alzheimer's Association, linked from the home page, offers a 24/7 helpline and local groups. A school counselor or a parent counts too. This guide cannot be that call.

Get certified Step 3 · A certificate is a record of learning

Step 3 · A certificate is a record of learning

Get certified

These are courses whose own pages, checked for this guide, say you can finish them and receive a certificate. A certificate shows you studied. It does not make you the doctor, and it does not by itself qualify you to lead a Pillar Project event. Prices and age rules can change, so read the page before you pay or sign up.

Want something to do with a person, rather than a course? The HQ Rec Room is the shelf of intergenerational activities.

Alzheimer's Association · public course

Understanding Alzheimer's and Dementia

Free. No age minimum is published, and the public courses are written for everyone. Finish it and download a certificate of completion from your Education Center dashboard. The Association says these public courses are not a professional certification. More free courses that use the same certificate steps are on the Education and Training Center.

Alzheimer's Association · essentiALZ

essentiALZ training and certification

$59.99 for one person, including the exam, with a year of access. Ages 18 and older, and written for professional care providers in long-term or community care. Pass the exam and you are certified in essentiALZ for two years.

Hilarity for Charity · youth

Brain Health Leader peer-educator training

Free, and online. For high school and college students in HFC's Youth Movement Against Alzheimer's. The youth page says you earn a certificate as a peer educator in brain health and Alzheimer's prevention. That is a youth leadership certificate, not a clinical caregiving license. Sign up from that page.

Alzheimer's Foundation of America

Partners in Care: Supporting Individuals Living With Dementia

$75. No minimum age is published on the course page. It is written for care partners and professionals. Complete the modules, pass the exam, and submit the evaluation to be certified as an AFA Dementia Care Partner. The credential is described on AFA's certifications page.

Dementia Friends USA

Become a Dementia Friend

Free. About 45–60 minutes online if you cannot attend a local session. No age minimum is stated on the national page. Watch the session, register, and receive an "I'm a Dementia Friend" certificate. That certificate marks community learning, not a professional license.

University of Tasmania · Wicking Dementia Centre

Understanding Dementia MOOC

Free, about 21 hours, and open with no closing date. You must be 18, or younger with a parent or guardian's consent, and the course is closed to anyone under 13. Finish the modules and the final quiz for a free certificate of completion. The course page says it is accredited by Alzheimer's Disease International. An optional paid premium certificate is also offered.

Johns Hopkins University · Coursera

Living with Dementia

Beginner course taught by Johns Hopkins instructors, about two weeks at a self-paced load. Coursera's terms prohibit anyone under 13, and some countries set a higher age for an account. The shareable certificate requires a paid enrollment. Financial aid is offered, and the course page does not list one fixed price.

Reflection prompts Step 4 · Not for a grade

Step 4 · Not for a grade

Reflection prompts

Write in a notebook, or use the printable. You do not have to share the answers. Skip any prompt that is too close to home today.

  1. 1. Which picture in the book actually helped — the cookie recipe, the word-orbs, the weedy roller coaster, the blocked New Data door — and which one feels too tidy for what you have seen?
  2. 2. ZAP does not clear the tangles. What does a family do with love that cannot reverse a disease?
  3. 3. Who carries the daily care in your life, or in Ava's (Hina, caring for Jayant)? What would one real hour of rest look like for them this week? Name the hour, not the feeling.
  4. 4. Co-regulation is shared calm, not control. When did you make a room quieter? When did you make it louder?
  5. 5. What boundary do you need — school, friends, sleep, not being the person who explains the diagnosis at dinner?
  6. 6. Is volunteering a fit (14 or older, waiver if you are under 18), or is the right-sized mission at the kitchen table? Write the smaller one if both feel true.

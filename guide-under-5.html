How to Read Case Alzheimer’s With Little Listeners

Case Alzheimer's is written for ages 6–12. This page is for families reading it aloud to a listener under 5. You aren't giving a biology lecture—you are taking them on a high-energy train and scooter ride inside the brain. When you read with big voices, dramatic pauses, and playful sound effects, big words like Hippocampus and Synaptic Gap stop feeling scary and start feeling like magic places on a map.

1. Page-by-Page Reading Guide 2. Brain Word Doodle Scavenger Hunt

Step 1: Shared Storytelling

Give Your Child the "Action Lines"

Before you open the book, tell your child: "I am the Narrator, and you are my Junior Sound & Action Agent!" Every time you reach one of these big speech moments in the book, point to the page and let your child shout or act out the line with full enthusiasm:

Brain HQ • Page 5

“NO! Not the cookies!”

Have your child put both hands on their head just like Agent H.M. when Big Boss says the Double-Chocolate-Chunk Cookie recipe is gone!

Synaptic Express • Pages 7–9

“SMACK!” & “SCREEEEEECHHH!”

Clap hands together on SMACK! when H.M. grabs the train rail, then lean back and make loud train brakes on SCREEEEEECHHH!

Signal Scooter • Pages 15 & 17

“ZOOM!!!” & “BEEP! SPUTTER... PFFFT!”

Pretend to hold scooter handlebars as you jump the Synaptic Gap, then blow a raspberry on PFFFT! when the ATP Fuel runs out.

Data Library & HQ • Pages 26–31

“EUREKA!” & “1... 2... 3... ZAP!”

Count to three together and press the giant orange button on the page with your fingers before shouting ZAP!

Step 2: Pause & Check Understanding

Page-by-Page Comprehension Checkpoints

Pause every few pages at these six story stops to ask a quick question and translate the story into toddler-friendly ideas.

Stop 1 • Pages 3–6 (Brain Map & Brain HQ) Focus: The Prefrontal Cortex & Missing Cookie Recipe
Ask Your Child: "Tap your forehead! That's where Brain HQ lives. What yummy recipe went missing in the glitch? Can you help Agent H.M. find it?"
How to Explain It: "Our brain is like a busy city with a Boss's office right behind our forehead. Sometimes in Alzheimer's, sneaky weeds called tangles get in the way and hide recipes or plans."
Stop 2 • Pages 7–9 (The Synaptic Express Train) Focus: Action Potential & Myelin Speed Boosters
Ask Your Child: "Look at Agent H.M. holding onto that super-fast white train! How fast do thoughts zoom inside your head?"
How to Explain It: "Every time you think or wiggle your toes, a tiny electric train called an Action Potential zooms down tracks wrapped in Myelin speed boosters!"
Stop 3 • Pages 11–14 (The Word Warehouse) Focus: Broca's & Wernicke's Areas, Aphasia & Tangles
Ask Your Child: "Point to the green vines wrapping up the colorful word balls! And look at the Manager—did you think the loud voice came from a giant monster or a tiny helper?"
How to Explain It: "Those round balls are words waiting to be spoken! When the green weeds (Tangles) wrap around them, it causes an Aphasia glitch—so Grandma might forget a word even when she knows what she wants to say."
Stop 4 • Pages 15–16 (The Big Jump on the Signal Scooter) Focus: Axon Terminal, Synaptic Gap & Neurotransmitters
Ask Your Child: "What are all those shiny blue bubbles floating next to their scooter as they jump across the gap?"
How to Explain It: "Brain cells don't touch! There is a tiny space between them called the Synaptic Gap. Those bubbles are Chemical Messengers (Neurotransmitters) carrying messages across!"
Stop 5 • Pages 17–22 (Emotion-Land & ATP Fuel) Focus: Amygdala & Cellular Energy
Ask Your Child: "Uh oh, the roller coaster in Emotion-Land has weeds on it and no one is smiling. What can we give someone we love to help them feel safe and happy again?"
How to Explain It: "Emotion-Land (the Amygdala) is where all our feelings live. When the rides get tangled, a grandparent might feel confused or grumpy—and our hugs and calm voices are like sparkling ATP Fuel!"
Stop 6 • Pages 23–34 (The Data Library & The Deep Archives) Focus: Hippocampus, Long-Term Memory & Empathy
Ask Your Child: "When they pressed the big button and shouted ZAP, did the weeds go away? Or did the golden screens play happy old movies instead?"
How to Explain It: "Even though the weeds block the New Data door so they might forget what happened today, their oldest, favorite memories are locked safely inside the Deep Archives forever!"

Step 3: The Brain Word Scavenger Hunt

Spot a Brain Word, Draw a Doodle!

🧠 Why Drawing Works: The Neuroscience of Visual Memory

In cognitive psychology and developmental neuroscience, young children learn through the Picture Superiority Effect and Dual-Coding Theory. Before age six, a child's abstract verbal networks are still developing, while their visual cortex and sensorimotor pathways are rapidly forming connections. When a child hears an unfamiliar scientific term like Hippocampus or Neurotransmitter and immediately draws a doodle of what they imagine it looks like, they encode the memory across three brain networks at once: auditory (hearing the word), visual (picturing the image), and kinesthetic/motor (moving the crayon). This triple-encoding anchors new concepts in long-term memory far more effectively than listening alone.
